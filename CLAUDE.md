# Frontier Sim: Project Brief

An open-source, top-down, systems-driven Old West game. The core is a simulated frontier economy that runs itself: settlers push west, find resources, sell them, and send goods back east. Money gets made, and other entities try to take it. Boom towns, ghost towns, dangerous trails, and bandit gangs emerge from simple rules.

## Purpose and success criteria

- This is a proof of concept for an idea, not a commercial product. It will be released open source so that someone with stronger art skills and assets can read the code, see how it's built, and potentially remake it at higher fidelity or scale.
- The demo works if a stranger can watch it for two minutes and see emergence: a town dying because the trail moved, a gang getting rich off one route until a posse forms.
- First measure of success is personal: does the simulated world produce stories worth watching?
- Code should be readable and well-structured, since other people are meant to study it. Prefer clarity over cleverness. Keep simulation logic separate from rendering so an artist or another developer can swap the presentation layer without touching the sim.

## Guiding principles

1. **Simulation first, game second.** The economy and agent systems are the hard, valuable part. Build and validate them headless before any graphics.
2. **Emergence from systems, not smart individuals.** Simple agents with needs, memory, and schedules interacting through shared world state.
3. **Traditional game AI, not ML.** Use utility AI (score actions by need and situation) and per-agent memory. Machine learning is only for offline parameter tuning, if at all. Every agent decision must be inspectable and debuggable.
4. **Experience shapes motivation.** Agents carry memory and adjust their own weights. A trader robbed twice on a route raises his risk aversion, hires an escort, or switches routes. A gang avoids a town that fought back. This is plain per-agent state feeding utility scores.
5. **Spirals are allowed when they would happen in the real world.** Boom/bust, overgrazing and the 1886-87 die-out, bison collapse, range-war escalation, and credit panics are all acceptable. Every collapse needs a traceable causal story rooted in the physical model, not numeric drift. Model the dampers too (scarcity pricing, migration, substitution, people giving up) so spirals run their course and sometimes recover.
6. **Log causes.** Keep an event log that records why things happened. This is the main tool for telling an emergent crash from a bug, and it later feeds player-facing rumors and headlines.
7. **Determinism.** Seeded generation and replayable runs, so bugs are reproducible.
8. **Legibility.** Emergence the player can't perceive might as well not exist. Plan to surface it through price boards, rumor feeds, visible caravans, and burned-out homesteads.

## Architecture: three layers

World size and simulation cost are separate. The world is large; detailed simulation is small and local.

1. **Strategic layer (whole map, always running).** A graph of nodes (towns, forts, railheads, mines, ranges) and edges (trails, rivers, rail). Each node holds aggregate state: population, stockpiles, local prices, cash balance, danger. Cheap enough to update daily across thousands of nodes. The economy lives here.
2. **Mobile entities on the graph.** Caravans, herds, gangs, and posses are single objects moving along edges, with a headcount and strength value. A gang of 12 is one object, not 12 simulated people.
3. **Tactical layer (near the player only, later).** Full individual detail and real-time encounters exist only in a bubble around the player. Off-screen events resolve statistically from strength, guards, and terrain, and the results are written to the event log. The aggregate model and local model must stay consistent so the player never returns to a town that contradicts what the sim said happened.

Use level-of-detail simulation: distant areas run as cheap aggregates, and only agents near the player get full behavior.

## Economic model (calibrate to history)

The frontier ran on arbitrage across distance plus heavy friction. Price gaps and freight costs should fall out of distance and bulk, so trade flows emerge without being scripted.

**Engines of activity**
- Extraction booms (mining, buffalo hides): sudden concentrated demand, sudden collapse when the resource thins. Supply merchants often out-earned the miners.
- Cattle: cheap in Texas, roughly ten times the value at northern railheads or stocking ranges after the Civil War. That gap paid for the drives.
- Government spending: forts and agency beef contracts anchor towns with steady cash and guaranteed buyers in remote places.
- Railheads: the biggest town-makers and town-killers. A town booms until the rails move on or quarantine lines push the trail west.
- Manufactured goods (flour, tools, whiskey, cloth, ammunition): cheap in the East, many times costlier far from rail.

**Frictions**
- Freight cost: ox-team wagons cover roughly 10-15 miles a day. Distance from rail translates directly into price. Steamboat to Fort Benton is a major northern link. Railroad arrival collapses local prices and kills old routes.
- Cash scarcity: credit, scrip, and IOUs carry much of the trade. Merchants and banks lend against cattle, land, and claims. This is where panics cascade.
- Time and risk: drives take months, with river crossings, stampedes, weather, disease (Texas fever quarantine shifts routes), and theft.
- Water and land control: whoever holds the water holds the range around it, the root of range wars and fencing disputes.

**Crime and law**
- Loot sources: cash payrolls, express shipments, stagecoaches, trains, herds. Rustling requires a market for the cattle.
- Law is funded by the economy: fee-based sheriffs and marshals, cattle associations paying detectives and brand inspectors. Where money flows, enforcement follows. Vigilantes appear where it doesn't.
- Crime follows value: violence clusters where cash, liquor, and transient men meet.

**Model layers**
1. Goods: start with 5-8 (beef cattle, flour/staples, tools, whiskey, ammunition, hides, ore, timber), each with base production cost and weight/bulk.
2. Nodes: local supply, demand, population, cash.
3. Routes: distance, terrain, danger, seasonal factor.
4. Agents: traders, drovers, settlers, bandits, lawmen, merchants. They move goods toward the biggest price gap, discounted by risk.
5. Shocks: weather and winter severity, disease, railroad extensions, strikes, resource depletion, as scheduled or random events on the physical layer.
6. Growth rules: settlements upgrade or attract population when food surplus, safety, and trade traffic cross thresholds, and decline when those fail.

Historical figures cited in design discussion were from memory and approximate. Verify prices, wages, freight rates, and trail distances against sources before calibrating.

**Time window (open decision).** 1866-1890 spans very different economies: early Texas-to-Kansas drives, the 1870s hide boom, and the 1880s northern bonanza ending in the 1886-87 winter. Either compress into one timeline with scheduled eras, or start with one slice. Suggested first slice: mid-1870s to mid-1880s drives and railheads.

## World generation

- Setting is real geography, from South Texas near the Mexican border to the Milk River in Montana. Use free elevation, river, and land-cover datasets (USGS or similar) for terrain.
- Generate the human layer procedurally: grazing quality from rainfall and terrain, ore from geology-flavored noise, timber and water from real data. Score locations for water, flat ground, resources, and trail proximity, and let the sim found towns where score and traffic justify them. Compute cheapest-path trails (river crossings are costs) and let traffic upgrade busy ones into roads.
- Hand-place a small number of key areas: real railheads, forts, major river crossings, sacred sites, and the map-edge weirdness. Procedural generation fills in between.
- Pre-simulate several decades of history before the player starts, so the world arrives with worn trails, ghost towns, and established ranches and gangs. This doubles as a tuning check: each generated history either looks plausible or it doesn't.
- Game time must be compressed relative to real travel time, with fast-forward through uneventful stretches. The cattle drive is a skippable delivery vehicle: random encounters and major stops only, wages drawn on arrival.

## Staged plan (each stage must be a complete, shareable thing)

1. **Headless sim.** Console program on the graph model from day one: 30-50 nodes, a few hundred aggregate agents, a handful of goods, traders and bandits, a price model, and an event log. Goal: see believable boom and bust in the log. Then scale node count to ~2,000 and check both performance and behavior.
2. **Overworld viewer.** Top-down map with towns and moving agents, placeholder art. Watching the world run is itself the first demo.
3. **One playable loop.** Simplest role first, probably cattle driver or trader, with real route and risk decisions (which route, which herd, whether to hire guards).
4. **Bandit and lawman roles.** New controllers over existing mechanics.
5. **Authored content layers.** Sacred sites, elders, map-edge weirdness, outlaw storyline. These are additive, not required for the core.
6. **Player-facing narrative systems.** Rumor feed and newspaper headlines generated from the event log. An LLM could write flavor text from the log, but only as description, never as control of world state.

If work stops after stage 2 or 3, something real still exists.

## Longer-term design intent (not for early stages)

- Roles: law enforcement, outlaw, cattle driver.
- Players can ride off from any stop for a few hours. Nearing the region edge gives a warning, then an Oregon Trail-style death cutscene. Far from towns and trails are secrets (buried treasure, mounds, weird or alien encounters) that evoke the settlers' sense of the strange. Music fades slowly to silence near the edge.
- Native American sacred sites act as safe spaces, with a rare, real, human elder or storyteller rather than a mystical apparition. Several distinct elder characters from different nations. These are hand-authored, never generated, and depiction should be culturally respectful and historically grounded.
- Outlaw storyline of aiding Native nations resisting expansion: a losing struggle in Red Dead Redemption 2's tone, where the player knows the outcome and the characters don't. The protagonist always has a self-serving or at least non-self-destructive reason to help. Cowboy-Native relations are shown as mutually beneficial rather than antagonistic.

## Ideas backlog (economy and agents)

- **Panic buying / hoarding.** A stock-out should itself raise demand (people stock up when shelves look empty), as the 2020 toilet-paper shortage showed. Model as a temporary demand multiplier at a node after a stock-out, with a traceable cause in the log and dampers (the surge exhausts itself, prices rise, traders respond).
- **Gold and the Army.** Gold backed the currency (silver coinage ended in 1873, specie resumption set for January 1879), so Washington had a stake in gold camps and the roads out of them, and the Army was often the only force on the frontier before the Posse Comitatus Act (June 1878) barred its use as a civil posse. Private express companies (Wells Fargo-style messengers) did most shipment guarding. Model patrols and escorts from nearby garrisons on gold routes when losses pass a threshold, with the 1878 restriction as a change in what the Army may do, and territorial governors or marshals as the ones who ask.
- **State and federal forces** (Texas Rangers, US Marshals, the Army) as the top rung of the law escalation ladder; today the Army and state capitals only count as "backup" that blocks gang capture.
- **Private armies with a label** (Johnson County War): an association labels small ranchers "rustlers" and invades.
- **Credit depends on who you deal with.** A draft or IOU from a far-off stranger is worth something to the Army or a big town, little to a remote camp, and nothing to a Comanchero. Acceptance should depend on the counterparty's trust and ties.
- **Information lag.** Traders currently see all prices with small noise. Make price knowledge travel with traders and telegraph lines.
- **Seasons.** River freight, winter on the trail, drive season.
- **Banks, credit and panics.** Cash scarcity is modelled; credit is not.
- **Mining in stages (Deadwood).** Placer gold draws a crowd of individual miners and plays out fast; then a large company arrives with outside capital for deep-vein work (Homestake, from 1877): fewer workers, a steady payroll, an industrial company town. Model as staged production per mine (placer, then lode), with the second stage started by outside capital and paid as wages into the town. Gambling as the town's later economy is outside the 1874-1886 window.
- **Merchant orders and freight houses (built).** Each town reviews its shelves weekly and, when cover falls below 40 days, posts an open order on its board for a good (cattle and horses included): quantity, a delivered price at the cheapest landed cost plus a margin, paid from the town's own cash on delivery. Traders see the order as certain revenue and now price several lot sizes (a thousand-head herd cannot go to a camp that eats seven a day), so a freight house with spare wagons or a drover can take it. An unfilled order's offer rises 25% a week up to 3 times the opening price and is withdrawn after 90 days; after 14 days the town hires its own freighters (the old convoy), so a thin market never leaves it to starve. Traders who get rich (more than $24,000) found freight houses: pooled capital, up to 12 outfits drawing an $8,000 float, broken outfits made good from house cash, the owner's memory of dangerous roads shared by every outfit, and a leader who weighs risk by the share of his wealth on one load (`house_nerve`); houses can close. Eastern firms found houses the same way. Results (20 seeds): about 340 traders at the end instead of 25, profit per trader $13-17 a day instead of $100-220, about 15 houses open at the end, East trade and money in circulation up. An open order is a floor, never a cap: it pays the higher of its offer and the town's shelf price (it used to pay far below a starving camp's price, which kept drovers away). Famine cause, found by experiment (20 seeds): the camps lacked beef. Holding beef on the shelf with flour untouched takes Deadwood from 462 hungry days to 0 and Helena from 101 to 0; no crime at all barely changes famine (59.5 vs 63.5 episodes) and a higher weekly order cap does not help. Without beef the sim makes people eat about 1.4 times the flour, which orders are not sized for, so flour runs out in lumps, hunger sets in, and a camp loses up to 30% of its people in a couple of months, regrows, and repeats. Beef was missing because merchants skipped cattle, drovers priced only full 1,000-head loads (a camp that eats 7 head a day collapses the price), and the order priced below the shelf. After the fixes famine episodes fell from 63.5 to 43.5, beef delivered to Deadwood rose from 5% to 83% of its need and its bare-shelf days fell from 85% to 28%. What the famine metric measures: shortage episodes (hunger average above 0.25), roughly IPC Phases 3-4. Nobody dies in the sim: hungry people leave (the target population shrinks by the hunger fraction and the excess walks away as drifters), so by the real definition (mortality of 2 per 10,000 a day, 30% of children malnourished) we have no famines. A death term is an option. Growth ignored food: arrivals came at 0.2% a day whenever the hunger average was low, so a boom town outgrew its supply and crashed 15-30% in a month, then regrew (Deadwood, 1-2 year cycle). Growth now slows as days of food on the shelf fall below `growth_cover_days` (30; civilians only), which removes that cycle at Deadwood but barely moves the famine count (20 seeds: 43.5, 44, 42 at 0/30/60 days): the remaining famines are steady-state shortages, mostly at Fort Laramie. Forts: the garrison used to eat from the town shelf, so traders bought the Army's flour and carried it to Deadwood, and the Army's flow was quietly feeding the civilians beside the post. Each post now has a government store (`Node.depot`) filled by contractors and Quartermaster trains (a train brings its usual share and tops the store up to `depot_floor_days` of cover); soldiers, agency rations and forage come from it first, traders cannot buy it, and the market shelf serves civilians only. Soldiers went from short 22% of days to 0%; a `garrison_short` event is logged. A famine now needs 100 or more civilians (a fort with a few dozen sutlers is not a town; the settlement should grow when merchant supply can carry it). Laramie's civilians still starve about 30% of days: it is the same thin-shelf merchant problem as Deadwood and Helena, at a smaller scale. Orders now size from each town's lead time (reorder point = days from the nearest eastern market plus `merchant_safety_days`, order up to that plus `merchant_cycle_days`), count only goods on the road as cover, and have no per-review cap (a town orders what it needs, as far as its cash goes); none of that moved famine either, and bigger buffers made it worse (49-52). Next: log a cause chain for each civilian famine (what was on order and for how long, what was on the road, what traders bought out in the last 30 days, days since the last delivery) and classify episodes across seeds instead of testing one theory at a time. Cause chain: every famine event now records, per food, the shelf cover, any open order and its age, freight on the road, what traders took off the shelf this month, and days since the last delivery. Classified over 20 seeds (1,186 starts): flour was already on the road in quantity (median 65 days of need) and arrived within three weeks in 82% of cases, so the shelf runs dry between lumps of freight; beef never reached the small forts (Laramie, Cheyenne, Miles City) in 61% of cases. Lead times were not the problem (first delivery after posting: Deadwood 6 days against 32 assumed). Deadwood and Helena are also cash-limited: median cash at a famine start was 42% and 27% of what a 117-day flour order costs. Why freight didn't come: on empty-shelf days Deadwood's flour price is at the 12x ceiling and a wagon of it nets about $2,100, while the freighters' best alternative nets about $6,600 (whiskey, ammunition and tools), so bulky staples lose the competition for wagon space; cash and risk are not what stops them. Inbound-aware pricing (traders price a town on shelf plus freight already on the road, less what it eats on the way; `inbound_awareness`, off by default) cut famines only slightly and shrank the economy badly (traders 330 to 116, population -6 to -21%), with or without contracts, so it stays off. Standing supply contracts (built, `contracts.cpp`): a town that went short within two years signs a contract with the freight house holding the most free capital: a fixed daily quantity that follows the town's civilian need, at the landed cost plus a 60% premium, delivered on a schedule from the house's own cash (each load is a convoy carrying the house's fear memory) and paid by the town on delivery as far as its cash goes (the rest is arrears). Robbed loads are made good; a town drops a house that falls 90 days behind; a contract runs a year. Army contracts are separate (the government store); these cover civilians only. Results (20 seeds): famine episodes 36.5 to 30, raw entries 76 to 52, East trade +5%, population +1.5%, traders unchanged, robberies +14%; Deadwood's raw famine starts fell 131 to 57 and Helena's 52 to 15, Fort Laramie's civilians went from starving 30% of days to 18% but it is still half of all remaining starts. Houses roughly break even on contracts (median margin about $15,000 a run), so the premium does not yet cover wages, guards and losses. Not yet: bundled runs that serve several towns on a corridor, a premium that follows the town's own shortage memory, houses expanding their fleets for contracts. Shortage versus famine: empty shelves are a shortage (the hunger average passes 0.25; people tighten belts and some leave); a famine is a spell of starvation that kills at least `famine_death_share` (10%, a guess) of a town's civilians. Deaths follow `deprivation`, a month-long average of unmet food need, above `starvation_threshold` (0.30) at up to `starvation_max_rate` (0.5% a day); soldiers eat from the government store and do not starve. Events are `shortage`/`shortage_over`, `starvation_began` (minor) and `famine`/`famine_over`. The batch metrics count only from `log_from` (they used to include the 1874 warm-up year, where about two thirds of the early famine events fell, so earlier absolute counts in this file were inflated; the comparisons between variants still hold). After warm-up (20 seeds, median): shortage episodes 30 without contracts, 21 with; famines 4 without, 2 with (range 0 to 5), people starved per run 1,666 without, 306 with. Famines at all are rare and sit at the small places: Cheyenne, Fort Laramie, Miles City. Deadwood and Helena lose people below the 10% line. Open: Cheyenne, a railhead, starves at all (its cash is small); local food (below). Local food: only farmers produce food in the model (0.05 cwt of flour-equivalent a day times soil yield; one farmer feeds about four people), plus ranch beef exports and the assumption that 80% of ranchers' and farmers' own diet is home-grown and untracked. Deadwood and Fort Laramie produce no farm food at all. Game (built): 22 towns have a herd (`game_capacity`, in head of beef-equivalent meat, 250 to 1,200; all guesses), 3% of the civilians hunt (`hunter_share`) and bring in about 20 lb a day each at full density (`hunter_catch`), what they take comes off the town's beef need before the shelf is touched, the herd regrows about 30% a year when thinned and strays in from outside, and a `game_thinned` event logs when it falls below 20%. Results (20 seeds, after warm-up): shortage episodes 21 to 18.5, famines 2 to 0 (median), people starved 306 to 213, population +2%; by the end about 78% of the game near these towns is hunted out (camps ate through their hills, as they did). Not modelled: separate species (black bear, deer, elk, antelope, rabbits and opossum are one pool, and opossum belong to the south and east), bison and their 1880s collapse, market hunters selling surplus to the shelf, seasons, gardens and foraging (unsure how historical), fishing. Famine work is parked at a level that reads as the cost of doing business on the frontier; open: Cheyenne, a railhead, still starves occasionally; deaths fall on everyone in a town equally, where real shortages fell on the poor first (people starve for lack of money as much as for lack of food), so deaths should follow wealth; houses only break even on contracts (the 60% premium does not cover wages, guards and losses); contracts are not yet bundled into runs serving several towns, and the premium does not follow a town's own shortage memory. Gaps: (1) beef still arrives at about 83% of need and in lumps, and the remaining famines follow; a camp eats only 4-12 head a day, so it cannot take a big herd quickly. Fix by herd stations: a drover or house parks a herd on grazing land near a camp and an employee sells it down against orders and the shelf price while the drovers go back for more (historical pattern, from secondhand sources, to verify; needs a grazing-capacity model, and a Missouri River route from Fort Pierre may be missing from the map); (1b) the remaining Deadwood flour supply is about 89% of nominal need with thin shelves (one to two weeks of cover against a 75-day target): an artificial flour top-up is not usable as evidence because it stops the merchants and traders responding; (2) the board alone fills only about half of what hired convoys did, and most orders are still filled by the town's own freighters after the wait; (3) hay and grain orders go almost entirely unfilled (too little margin for a trader); (4) crime rose about five times when houses arrived (whole-load robberies about 3,800, posses about 770 a run); (5) freelancer arrivals quadrupled because house outfits make many 'success stories', and about 380 lone traders a run go broke, so entry needs calibrating to profit, not to rare big wins; (6) the drover share fell from about 60% to 20%; (7) orders ignore information lag and credit; each wagon of an order still has its own guards (a train should pool them); (8) the floats, founding cash and the 12-outfit cap are guesses. Famine is counted as merged episodes plus distinct towns.
- **Gangs take what they can carry (built).** A gang judges a load by what the goods are worth (not the trader's freight-inflated cost) and takes what fits on saddles (about 60 lb a man; livestock by head); gold is taken whole. A stop must yield about $5 a man, so flour, timber and hides are never worth a fight, and a partial take is a toll (the wagons go on). Food is taken only by a gang with under 5 days of cash. All numbers are rough. Results: whole-load robberies down about two thirds, merchant goods robbed down about 85%, posses halved (houses later raised crime again, see above). Gaps: (1) gangs have no needs: add a daily whiskey and food ration drawn from the hideout's stock (dry means infighting and desertion; a starving hideout makes the leader move the gang or lose men, since gangs have no tie to a town beyond what it supplies), and ammunition spent only in fights (more when the party is guarded) plus a small practice trickle for a capped training bonus, with a gang out of ammunition taking ammunition first; (2) `herd_skill`: only gangs with livestock experience should rustle; (3) outrage and posse response are flat per robbery, so a $6M gold robbery counts the same as a $90 toll and should scale with the loss; (4) trader risk estimates are per trail, not per good, so a flour trader still fears a road made dangerous by gold robberies; (5) a 500 lb cotton bale is not saddle freight; (6) the low trader count was the starting number, not a loss of traders, and the freight houses now carry it.
- **Freight jobs, shocks and entry from need (built; `jobs.cpp`).** Finding: an outfit paid the full freight rate on departure (to teamsters the model never pays) on top of its own upkeep, so every owner-driver paid for his own wagon twice, a likely reason most lone traders lost money; carriers now skip that charge. Under the old merchant model a flour wagon earned less than an ammunition wagon and the median loaded leg lost for most goods (flour about -$205 a trip, 60% of flour trips lost; 79% of lone traders ruined). A freight job is the town's open order carried on the supplier's goods: the supplier fronts them, the town pays goods plus haul from its cash on delivery, the carrier is paid by draft on delivery (nothing earned rides on the road), and a robbed load is the supplier's loss (the carrier earns nothing for the trip but is not charged for the goods). Pay is `job_rate_share` (0.25) of the freight rate on the weight and miles, scaled up as the order goes unfilled (the escalation) and capped at the order's spread. `carrier_jobs` 2 lets lone traders, house outfits and eastern firms take them (1 leaves out firms; firms haul about 60% of jobs and leaving them out cuts most of the benefit; 0 is off). Results (20 seeds, median): shortage episodes 18.5 to 13, starved 213 to 134, famines 0, population +4%, traders at the end 334 to 617, robberies +23% (per outfit robberies fell; gangs formed +2%), flour trips +$456 instead of -$205 (15% lose), lone traders ruined 79% to 62%, still trading at the end 1% to 14%. Paying the whole spread (v1) was far too rich (trader cap hit, money +140%). Shocks are scenario entries, off in the default scenario: `road_closure` (trail and river edges touching listed towns shut for N days; wagons wait at the last stop), `house_collapse` (the largest freight house loses its head; contracts lapse, convoys are lost, `fraction` of its outfits are lost and the rest go independent), `firm_withdrawal` (eastern outfits finish their load and quit, eastern houses close). Measured against the same seeds without the shock: a 45-day blizzard into the Black Hills costs Deadwood about 285 extra deaths (shelf empty by day 45, flour 6x, then a glut at 0.2-0.5x); a 60-day northern winter about 700 (worst seed 900); the largest house collapsing or every eastern firm leaving changes almost nothing (the system has redundant supply: town-hired convoys, speculative traders, jobs), and a winter plus a firm withdrawal is about the winter alone (worst seed about 1,150, around 10% of Deadwood). Lone traders did not grow into a gap on their own: entry followed success stories, not need. Entry now also follows hunger (`entry_scarcity_weight` 1.0): the rise of the share of towns in shortage above its one-year average adds to the rumor. Escalated orders were tried as the signal and are useless (some town is always waiting; the talk did not move when a third of the freight left). With it a winter costs 482 extra deaths instead of 698, and normal runs barely change (traders +6%). Gaps: jobs are found only at the market where an outfit stands (deadhead planning ignores job pay); the town's cash limit is unchanged (Deadwood and Helena hold 42% and 27% of a 117-day flour order); the town's payment east is not carried by courier or Wells Fargo; town-hired convoys still run beside the job board; the 0.25 share and the pay rule are judgment calls; the shocks are synthetic (dates, lengths and towns are guesses; a real blizzard slowed and killed stock more than it closed every road); random scheduled shocks for the game side are not built; the baseline-versus-shock comparison measures deaths, not the 10% famine line.
- **Scale test and the trading horizon (built).** A synthetic world (the 30 real nodes plus farm and ranch towns scattered across the same map, each linked to its three nearest neighbours; trader counts scaled with the node count) showed the cost of a day grew with the square of the map: 400 days took 1.4 s at 30 nodes, 9 s at 100 and 85 s at 300 (about 9.5 s a day extrapolated to 2,000 nodes, 11 hours for a run). Causes: the daily table of the best load waiting at each town walked every route between every pair of towns and priced every good (about 55% of the time at 300 nodes), every trader's buy and sell planner scanned every town, and every merchant order searched every node for a source. Fix: a trader considers only the nearest `trade_horizon` (64) towns by travel time plus every reachable eastern market (`horizon()`), the route miles and days to each are cached when the network changes, and the best-load table prices each good's load once instead of once per destination. At 30 nodes the horizon covers the whole map and results are bit-identical (same hashes, all tests pass). After: 400 days take 4 s at 100 nodes, 12 s at 300, 24 s at 600; 100 days take 14 s at 1,200 and 26 s at 2,000 (0.26 s a day, about 40 times faster than before at 2,000 nodes). Full 12-year runs: 300 nodes 3.6 min, 600 nodes 6.6 min, so about 20-25 minutes is expected at 2,000 (not run in full). Memory is small (94 MB at 2,000). The big synthetic worlds stay sane (11 shortage episodes and no famines across 300-600 towns; population 2.6 to 3.7 times its start, since the synthetic farm towns are generous), but they are a cost test, not a calibrated map. Next cost at scale: per-trader noise draws for every node (rng calls grow with the map) and the merchants' daily review. Tried and parked: a winter store (`winter_cover_days`, off): northern towns raise their reorder point and order-up-to target by a month of cover from September to February. It made the 60-day winter worse (658 extra deaths against 482), and the shelf at the shock date was lower, not higher, probably because Deadwood is cash-limited and the larger orders compete for the same money; it also added crowding in normal runs (traders +12%, robberies +16%). A fix likely needs the town's cash (credit, or a draft on the supplier) rather than a bigger target.

- **Gang camps (first pass built; `camp_stages` 1, crime.cpp).** Why: a gang that lives and works inside a town is found by that town's law and drains its cash, and a careful outlaw would not make trouble where he sleeps. Model: when a gang picks its trail it also gets a camp (`Gang::camp_miles`, 15 to 50 miles up the trail from the town it trades with, never more than 45% of the trail; `camp_kind` is a label for the viewer: dugout, cabin whose owners were driven off, abandoned cabin, ruined blockhouse, line shack, canyon with water; the sim never reads it). The town it trades with (`hideout`) still fences loot, sells supplies, supplies recruits and receives upkeep. What changed: (1) no daily catch roll in town for bands (that was `band_petty_catch`); instead lawmen find the camp at `camp_found_catch` 0.003 a day (times law and fame); (2) a flush gang (cash over `camp_visit_cash_per_man` $150 a man) rides in with `camp_visit_chance` 0.08 a day and leaves `camp_visit_spend` 0.5 of its cash in town, which also feeds the town-capture influence; (3) each boss has a carefulness drawn when his gang forms (`Gang::care`: 10% careless, 10% careful, 80% ordinary, separate from `competence`), and a visit ends in trouble with chance 0.3 / 0.1 / 0.02 (`camp_trouble_careless` / `_ordinary` / `_careful`): the town gets `camp_trouble_outrage` 1.5 and the gang some fame; (4) a band is caught on a visit at `camp_visit_catch` 0.05 (three times that after trouble) and when it sells loot at `camp_fence_catch` 0.002 a robbery; (5) a would-be catch on the road ends with the band reaching its camp at `camp_escape` 0.3. Bands still form where the idle men are (Austin, San Antonio and Fort Worth lead), but they no longer live there. Second round (large-gang pipeline), all measured at 31 nodes (10 seeds, medians): (1) **Heat-based findability.** Lawmen find a camp only by following a trail of hold-ups: each deed adds heat (`Gang::heat`, `camp_heat_per_fame` 2 x the deed's fame; bushwhacking `camp_heat_bush` 0.2), heat halves every `camp_heat_halflife` 30 days, a band that moves keeps `camp_heat_move` 0.25 of it, and the daily camp-found chance (`camp_found_catch` 0.01) is scaled by heat over `camp_notice_ref` 3 (floor `camp_notice_floor` 0.1) and by 1 / (1 + camp miles / `camp_far_miles` 40), so a far camp is harder to link. - **Jail, a record and the rope (built; `jail_stages` 1, crime.cpp `gang_arrest`, `jail_day`).** Why: a band the law caught used to vanish (half its men scattered back to the fighter pool, half were simply gone), so a first petty offence ended a band for good and nothing connected the men in a town's jail to the next band. Model: where there is law a caught band's men are jailed at the nearest town; a band with no hold-up or killing behind it serves `jail_petty_min`-`jail_petty_max` (5-30) days, a band that robbed `jail_job_min`-`jail_job_max` (60-180), a man with a record `jail_repeat_mult` (2) times longer, never more than `jail_cap_days` 365, and a marshal's lockup holds men at most `jail_marshal_cap` 30 days (the sheriff's county jail the full term). A band with `jail_hang_kills` 2 killings or `jail_hang_jobs` 8 hold-ups risks hanging (`jail_hang_chance` 0.5, doubled if its men have a record): `jail_hang_share` 0.4 of the men are hanged, the leader among them, and the rest serve the year. Where there is no law (`Node.law` 0) there is no jail: the town runs the men out (they scatter, no record). Men serving time are in `Node.jailed` (release day, men); on release they join the fighters at that town and count as `Node.ex_cons`, a share that fades with the fighters. Bands form from the fighters in proportion, so a new band carries men with a record (`Gang::record_men`), each credited `ex_con_fights` 10 fights, which makes him a veteran (1.5 men in a fight, helps his boss hold men); records merge when bands join. `Gang::kills` counts killings in hold-ups and ambushes. Log events: `band_jailed`, `band_hanged`, `band_run_out` (headlines for hangings and for jailed bands of 3 or more jobs). Men ledger rows: jailed, let out with a record, hanged. Sources (a web search this session; findings are thin, numbers are guesses within them): a summary of an NCJRS abstract on frontier jails (read only as a search snippet, not the page) says they were small, usually holding one to five people, that no typical frontier jail existed, that some were holding facilities, and that in mining camps jail sentences were almost unknown because camp law banished offenders; a Dodge City police docket page shows a drunk-and-disorderly fine of $2 plus costs in 1878, and a Kansas ordinance of 1877 set fines of $1 to $25 for public drunkenness, neither mentioning jail time, so fines were the usual penalty for petty offences; Butch Cassidy got two years for grand larceny in 1894 and served 18 months at the Wyoming prison in Laramie; Helena's vigilantes executed at least 14 men in 1865-70 and Granville Stuart's Montana Stranglers killed between 19 and 35 alleged horse thieves in 1883-84; Dodge City and the other Kansas railheads had 45 homicides in 1870-85, 16 by peace officers. NOT found: typical jail terms for petty theft or vagrancy, the share of caught robbers hanged, how often men were released to rejoin gangs. Books to check: McFarland's *Crime, Justice and Retribution in the American West, 1850-1900* (2017) and the Nevada frontier crime and punishment study (1859-1878). Results (20 seeds, wars on against off): gangs on the road 9.3 to 6.9, men per gang unchanged (13), largest gang 30 to 46 (median), but gang-days at 30+ men unchanged (about 245), about 290 gang fights, 60 mergers and 17 breakdowns a run, robberies -5%, posse victories unchanged, town takeovers 4 to 2. Not yet: the Army/detective response does not scale with a gang's size or wins, rival fights ignore hideout and town control (two gangs claiming one town), the player's own gang has no special rules, all numbers are guesses. Next test: whether a gang that beats posses draws a bigger response, and whether the break-up leaves drifters that re-form smaller gangs (the cycle).
- **Two-tier gangs: small bands and a few large gangs (first pass built; `gang_tiers` 1, crime.cpp).** Why: the first gang model formed ~780 gangs a run from drifters, because every idle man was a potential bandit (the men-flow ledger in the batch report showed half of all drifters ending in gangs, plus 3,700 men recruited from nowhere). History (sources found, no counts): famous gangs rode in parties of 3 to 14, lasted months to a few years, had fluid membership and a wider circle of helpers; Hole-in-the-Wall was several gangs sharing one hideout; the Cochise County Cowboys (~100 men by 1879) were a loose association. No source found for how many gangs a region held or how long outlaw careers lasted. Model: idle men turn to crime at a small daily rate (`fighter_turn_rate`) and become fighters at the town (`Node.fighters`); fighters give up at `fighter_decay`; 2-5 of them form a band (`band_form_chance`) living on petty theft from the hideout town's cash (`band_petty_take`) and on hold-ups of wagons they expect to beat (`band_min_odds`); each hold-up and each day risks lawmen catching the band (`band_catch_base`, `band_petty_catch`, law fund raises it up to `band_law_cap`). A band with `band_merge_min_robberies` jobs behind it joins a gang in reach that has room (or pools with another such band): the gang keeps its raiding parties (`cells`, up to `gang_party_max` men each) and robs as parties but fights a posse as one body. Large gangs recruit only from fighters at their hideout. Posses and detectives are sized to `law_overmatch` times the gang's men; a hardened gang counts `gang_hardness` over townsmen. Two small bands never fight each other. One 10-man gang is seeded at Fort Griffin. Results (20 seeds, tiers on against off): bands on the road 1 to 20, men per gang 2.2, large gangs only 0.17 on the road on average (goal was about 1), largest gang 15, about 1,080 bands formed a run (they last about 78 days), 530 caught, 39 joins; robberies down 65% (4,070 to 1,430), traders at the end sit at the 1,500 cap in most runs (median 755 before), town takeovers by gangs fell from 3 to 0, posses win about 85% of the time. Large gangs were rare and short-lived (0.17 on the road) until the law was thinned (next bullet). Open: the economic effect of far less crime (the trader cap hides it), gang takeovers of towns, bands and large gangs look the same in the viewer, and all numbers are guesses.
- **Law by stages, infamy, newspapers and bushwhacking (first pass built; `law_stages`, `fame_stages`, `news_stages`, `bush_stages`).** Why: the first two-tier model killed every large gang in a few weeks (10 seeds: law at a quarter strength gave 0.36 large gangs on the road, no law at all gave 3.9 with a largest of 33), because law was money in every town and every posse knew every gang. Facts used (from memory, to verify: Dykstra, *The Cattle Towns*; Prassel, *The Western Peace Officer*; McGrath, *Gunfighters, Highwaymen and Vigilantes*; Anderson and Hill, *The Not-So-Wild, Wild West*; Shinn, *Mining Camps*): mining camps ran on miners' meetings before any officer; Deadwood in 1876 sat on treaty land with no legal government (Lawrence County organized 1877); cattle-town police were paid from fines and vice licences and followed the season; jurisdictions did not share much, and crossing a line was a real tactic that faded as telegraph and railway express spread. Model: (1) **Regions** (`region` per node in world.json, territories and states by real border; `regions` lists an `organized` date, the Black Hills 1877-01-01 as a guess, Indian Territory never). **Law level** per town: 0 none, 1 marshal, 2 county sheriff and deputies (`law_day`, law.cpp): a marshal when civilians pass 150 for 120 days, or at once after outrage 3 in a camp of 60 or more; a sheriff at 500 civilians once the region is organized; a post has at least a marshal; law lapses 240 days after the town falls below half the threshold. No law means no tax set aside (`law_fund` stays empty), no posses (the citizens may raise a vigilance committee after outrage, which makes its leader marshal if it wins), and a marshal's posse is capped at 8 men. Money only pays for law once it exists. A town with no law also has no interest backing (`has_backup` shrinks to 40% of the range), so a large gang can take it over. About 8% of town-days have no law. (2) **Infamy and flight** (crime.cpp): each gang keeps a `known` value per region (deeds add `fame_robbery` 0.5, toll 0.15, kill 0.5, a won fight 0.3; fades with a 140-day half-life; papers add more elsewhere). A town raises a posse only against a gang its region knows (`fame_posse_min` 1.0); catch chances scale with fame (0.3x unknown to 2x famous) and with `law_presence` (0.15 none, 0.6 marshal, 1 sheriff); a trail's attraction is divided by the gang's heat (fame times size), and a gang whose heat passes `fame_flee_trigger` rides up to `gang_flee_range` 450 miles to fresh country (`gang_fled`, about 21 in an eight-year run). (3) **Newspapers** (news.cpp): a town gets a paper after 180 days at 800 civilians (23 by the end); log entries become one-line template headlines (robbery over $1,500, takeover, posse routed, famine, ghost town, rush, murdered officer...) that reach papers within a range scaled by weight (`news_range` 900 miles, `news_speed` 60 miles a day), and each print adds fame in that paper's region. A paper's recent file is `Simulation::paper_headlines`; the viewer's town panel shows it. (4) **Bushwhacking**: a band also waylays lone men (`bush_rate` 0.02 a day): a resident who is killed is missed or found in 2-8 days and angers his town (outrage +2); a stranger adds only 0.05 outrage and a little fear; 1% of victims are notable (an officer or a mine owner), which gives a 'Horror Scene' headline, outrage 4 and, if a witness names the gang, a cavalry detachment from a post within 300 miles (`PosseKind::Army`). (5) **Thinner law** (the numbers that moved large gangs): `law_overmatch` 3 to 2, citizen posses worth 0.6 of a gang man (varies 0.5x to 1.5x, `posse_base_skill`), `posse_outrage_threshold` 2 to 8, `posse_cooldown` 30 to 120, a town whose posse was routed waits 90 days, agencies send 4 to 12 men at skill 1.5 and wait 365 days between operations (loss threshold 6,000), `law_deter_scale` 1,500 to 4,000, `band_catch_base` 0.015. Results (20 seeds, median): bands on the road 22 (was 20), large gangs on the road 1.25 (was 0.17), largest gang 20 (15), about 62 gangs a run reach 8 men and last about 143 days from formation, about half of them ending to a posse, vigilance or detectives; robberies 2,560 (1,430), traders at the end 1,360 (the 1,500 cap no longer saturates), takeovers 0.25 a run (0), 1,800 bushwhackings a run (470 killed, 17 notable, 4 cavalry detachments), 7,650 headlines. Not yet: Army and federal marshals as a general top rung, the player's gang and law roles, wanted posters and rewards, fame spread by travellers (papers only), regions are hand-set (not derived from borders), the 2,000-node scale test with these systems, and the numbers (all guesses). Dev tool: `law_probe` prints every town's law, region and paper over time.
- **Farms and new towns (first pass built).** Farmers settle where the farm-gate price (best of local market and any outlet worth the freight) beats break-even; weather is drawn per zone per year; grain buyers ship surplus to the East by rail. Latent sites (Spearfish Valley, Garden City, Billings) become towns when settling pays for several months. Gaps: Texas has no outlet for grain (no Gulf market, so its farm districts shrink to local demand); farm numbers, capacities, opening dates and break-even price are rough and must be verified against sources; towns still cannot be founded by traffic alone (stage stops, crossings, mining strikes); farm districts do not yet feed an Indian agency or fort by direct contract with local farmers.

## Known risks

- Scope creep toward the full open-world vision, which is team-sized.
- Time is the real cost: systems-heavy solo games take years, and tuning never fully ends.
- Economic sims can run fine mechanically and still be boring, or collapse for unintended reasons.
- Interface work to make emergence legible is easy to underestimate.
- Staying motivated through the long middle, when the sim works but isn't fun yet. Mitigation: get a watchable version early.

## Decisions made

- **Language:** C++20 with CMake. `sim/` is a dependency-free static library; `cli/` is the headless runner; a future viewer (likely raylib) links only against `sim`. Config in JSON (nlohmann/json, header-only). Own seeded PRNG (xoshiro256**), not `<random>` distributions, for cross-compiler determinism.
- **Viewer:** raylib (zlib licence), an optional CMake target (`-DFRONTIER_BUILD_VIEWER=ON`, off by default so `sim/` and the CLI build with no dependencies), fetched at configure time. `viewer/main.cpp` reads the sim only through its const accessors and event log. First pass: top-down map in the sim's own coordinates, towns sized by population, roads tinted by recent robberies, wagon trains moving along their roads (colored by freelancer, house outfit, eastern firm, freight job), gangs on their roads, food shortages ringed, a town panel (shelf, price, open orders), overlays (kind, hunger, flour price), a non-routine event feed with causes, a headline panel of the big stories (click one to jump to its town), carrier filter (C) and loaded-only (L), click a wagon to follow it and read its cargo, destination and fear, speed and pause controls, and `--shot FILE --days N` to render one frame headless. No terrain yet: that needs the world generator, which is a separate piece of work.
- **License:** Apache 2.0 (art and authored content may be licensed separately later).

## Working agreements for Claude Code

- Keep the simulation core free of rendering and engine dependencies, so it runs headless and can be tested on its own.
- Make all randomness seeded and runs replayable.
- Every significant world event writes a cause-tagged entry to the event log.
- Prefer data-driven definitions (goods, routes, agent types, shock events) in config files over hard-coded values, so balance can be tuned without recompiling and contributors can read the model.
- Write short comments explaining why a rule exists in economic or historical terms, since others will study this code.
- When something behaves strangely, check the event log for the causal chain before changing numbers.
- Build in stages. Don't start a later stage until the earlier one produces believable behavior.
- Keep `Changelog.md` current: add an entry (plain language, with the commit hash) with every commit, newest first, so a newcomer can see where things stand.
